The Google Sheets almanac

A record book for what happened in your league

The downloadable, open-source project builds a Google Sheets Almanac for your league. The guided setup currently supports Windows and ESPN points leagues, both season-points and head-to-head.

It produces all-time standings and records, matchup and draft history, a page for every team, a trade board, and other views built around what actually counted in your fantasy league, not just the MLB stats your players accumulated along the way.

Explore any slice of league history

The demo runs a sample ESPN league with fictional club and owner names and real MLB production. It runs entirely in your browser: standings, team pages with a lineup diamond or table, player statistics, and filters that recompute on the page. There's nothing to install and no account.

The data comes from the same pipeline as the workbook. Filters change what you’re looking at without affecting the underlying data, so you can present your data however you like.

Team pages cover the current roster and club history. Superteams lets you build a lineup from the top performers across your chosen clubs and seasons. The guide explains their filters and controls, along with the plans for pages still taking shape.

Early preview: the first load on a slow connection can take several seconds. Faster loads are planned for 2.3.

Shared data

What it is built on

The dashboard is the visible part. The valuable work is making the historic data trustworthy.

Fantasy platforms leave years of roster and lineup history behind. The models reconstruct it from transaction logs, season endpoints, scoring settings, and the other evidence that survives, recovering far more of the league than any single source exposes. Some eras are still fuzzier than others, and the Almanac labels those limits instead of laundering estimates into facts.

dbt brings those sources into a shared model. The workbook and dashboard are two presentations of that underlying data; the dashboard does not read the Sheets workbook. Scoring definitions, totals, and display rules travel with the data so the browser can use the same interpretation.

Why I made it

Treat the league like a real competition with its own history

What player has spent the most time on your team over the years? Which owner consistently finds value late in drafts? What was the biggest trade in league history? Which players can a franchise reasonably claim as “their guys”? Just how badly does our one friend in St. Louis overrate Cardinals players?

Fantasy baseball platforms retain much of the underlying data needed to answer these questions, but do not make it easy to access natively.

It is more toy or museum than analytic toolbox, but it does provide unique views of your league that you can act on.

Current-season views help spot roster strengths, positional holes and plausible trade partners. Get a quick view, for example, of which team has had the worst shortstop production this year, or which has catcher-eligible points accruing on its bench. If your league allows pick trading, the Draft Recap gives an idea what those picks are actually worth.

The weekly recap was where this started: keep the fun of writing league updates, let the Almanac handle the bookkeeping. It’s currently formatted for ESPN’s stone-age front-page editor: the week’s best and worst performances, the points left on benches, the records broken, and whichever configurable oddities happened to fire that week. Think a lucky win for the week’s second-lowest scorer, or one player single-handedly accounting for his team’s margin of victory.

I made it for my own leagues, got encouraging feedback, and eventually cleaned it up enough that somebody who didn’t build it could use it. It’s also become sort of a professional project to sharpen some data-engineering skills. It’s free and open source. There’s no “Almanac account” and nothing to pay for.
